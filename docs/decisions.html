Decision log

How the analysis was defined, and the evidence behind each choice

Published

October 26, 2026

Every definition in this project was a choice. This log records each one: the question, the options, the evidence, and where the decision came from. Basketball marks decisions that came from how the game is played; Statistics marks decisions driven by the data or the model. All numbers below are computed from the data when this page is rendered (NBA regular seasons 2023-24 and 2024-25); see Data notes for the validation of the raw sources.

1 What counts as “pushing after a make”

1.1 Only possessions after a made field goal, not after a made free throw

Origin: rules. After a made field goal the game clock keeps running during the inbound; after a free throw it is stopped until the inbound is touched (NBA Rule 5). Time to the first action would include the inbound in one case and not in the other, so only made field goals are used.

1.2 Intention is a continuous pace, not a fast-break count

Origin: Basketball, confirmed by the data. The first definition was the FIBA fast-break rule (first action within 8 s). It turned out to describe a fast break, not the decision to push: a team that pushes after a make usually ends in early offense, around 8–12 s, and a push that the defense stops looks like any long possession.

With only about 5% of possessions as strict fast breaks, a per-game fast-break rate would rest on about two possessions. The average time to the first action uses every possession and separates teams clearly. Decision: intention is the team-game average time to the first action, with no cut-off fixed in advance.

1.3 The inbound is part of pushing

Origin: Basketball. The moment the ball is touched in bounds is not in any public data, so the inbound cannot be removed. It should not be: inbounding quickly is part of pushing. The part every team takes is a constant that cancels out of the within-team comparison.

1.4 Time to first action, not possession length

Origin: Statistics. Possession length is also available, but it grows with offensive rebounds and ends at the defensive rebound after a miss, which is sometimes logged late. It is kept as a robustness check.

2 When the clock, not the team, sets the pace

2.1 The last 54 seconds of quarters 1–3 are excluded

Origin: Basketball, confirmed by the data. Near the end of a quarter, teams shoot early to get a 2-for-1, hold for the last shot, or heave. Time to the first action then reflects the clock, not a choice to push.

2.2 The last 2:00 of the 4th quarter and of overtime are excluded

Origin: rules. The game clock stops after made baskets there, so the inbound is no longer inside the time to the first action.

2.3 Garbage time follows Cleaning the Glass, without the starters condition

Origin: Statistics. The margin thresholds of Cleaning the Glass are applied (4th quarter only: 25, 20 and 10 points by time left). Its second condition, two or fewer starters on the floor, cannot be applied because the data have no lineups, so slightly more possessions are excluded.

3 Separating the score from the choice to push

3.1 Three analyses: full, close and first quarter

Origin: Basketball. Teams behind push to catch up and teams ahead slow down, so the score can drive pace. The same model is fitted on all possessions, on close possessions, and on first quarters only, where the game starts tied and the plan is not yet shaped by the score.

3.2 The score moves the pace, inside every team

Origin: Basketball, confirmed by the data. The first fit of the main model, on all possessions, gave a small negative return: in games where a team played faster than usual after makes, those possessions went slightly worse. Teams that are behind speed up to catch up, so the games that go badly are also the faster ones. That alone can produce a negative association.

The correlation cannot separate “behind, so faster” from “faster, it went wrong, so behind”: the margin at each possession already includes the earlier possessions. That is why the model is also fitted on close possessions and on first quarters, where the score has had little time to build up.

3.3 “Close” means within 5 points, in quarters 1–3

Origin: Basketball, confirmed by the data. Close games are played slower near the end, long before the last two minutes.

Keeping the 4th quarter would make a game look slow partly because it was close at the end. “Close” is measured at the start of each possession, never by the final result: selecting games by how they ended would condition on the outcome.

4 What a possession is worth

4.1 Net points: the cost of how a possession ends

Origin: Basketball and Statistics. A turnover costs more than the points not scored: it hands the opponent a better possession. Each possession is charged with the value of the opponent’s next possession, estimated within the same season.

A live-ball turnover hands the opponent about 0.2 points more than a made basket does. That is the cost the net points charge.

5 Measuring intention with error

5.1 The game’s average pace is a noisy measurement

Origin: Statistics. A team-game has about 30 possessions after a made basket, and time to the first action varies a lot within a game. Its average is therefore measured with error.

Using the observed average directly as a predictor would bias the estimated return towards zero. Decision: the true pace of each game is a latent parameter, measured by the observed average with this known error.

5.2 Team-game likelihood

Origin: Statistics. Net points per 100 of a team-game are an average of about 30 possessions, close to normal by the central limit theorem, with a spread that shrinks with the number of possessions. A possession-level model is a planned extension.

6 Seasons

6.1 Every quantity is paired with its own season

Origin: Basketball. Rosters and coaches change between seasons and within them. The unit is the team-season, and no number from one season is used to describe or adjust another.

6.2 2025-26 is descriptive only in this version

Origin: data availability. Possession-level data for 2025-26 are not in the public dump used here. The season of Miami’s change of pace after Jimmy Butler’s departure is described with team totals from pbpstats.com; adding it to the model needs a possession parser for the new play-by-play format, planned as the first update.

 

Independent analysis using public data, presented as an example case; not affiliated with the NBA or any of its teams.
